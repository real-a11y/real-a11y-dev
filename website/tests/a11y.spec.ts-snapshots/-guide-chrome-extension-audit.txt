  heading "Semantic Navigator" (level 1)
  paragraph "Navigate the web the way your users have to."
  paragraph "Most accessibility problems don't look like problems — until you remove the visual layer. Semantic Navigator replaces the page with its accessibility tree. You can still click, navigate, fill forms, and complete tasks. The structure is all you get."

  heading "The problem" (level 2)
  paragraph "You build, test, and ship products you have never used the way a screen reader user does."
  paragraph "You navigate with your eyes: you click what you can see and skip what doesn't look relevant. Your users navigate with structure — headings to orient, landmarks to jump to, roles and names to understand what each element does. They hear the tree, one element at a time, in the order the DOM gives it to them."
  paragraph "Most teams have never used their own product that way. Not because they don't care, but because nothing has ever made them try."
  heading "The test" (level 2)
  paragraph
    strong "If you can complete the task through the accessibility tree, your users probably can too."
  paragraph
    strong "If you can't — that's the bug."
  paragraph "Try to check out. Try to book an appointment. Try to find the error message after a failed form submission. Try to close the dialog that just opened. Do it through the tree, without looking at the UI."
  paragraph "When you can't find the button, can't tell what a control does, or can't tell whether the action worked, stop there. That's your accessibility bug, and your screen reader users hit it every day."
  heading "This is not a replacement for real testing" (level 2)
  paragraph "NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver and TalkBack are the tools your users depend on, each with its own keyboard model and its own way of announcing dynamic content. None of that is simulated here. Semantic Navigator shows the raw material those tools read, the semantic tree, not what a screen reader says or how it moves."
  paragraph ": missing names, broken tab order, wrong roles, unlabeled landmarks. : NVDA with Firefox, VoiceOver with Safari, JAWS if your users depend on it. This extension makes those sessions less painful. It does not make them optional."

  heading "The screen curtain" (level 2)
  paragraph "The button in the panel's toolbar blacks out the whole page. Only an extension can paint over a whole browser tab."
    strong "Curtain"
  paragraph "The tree stays. The panel stays. You can still use the full product. The visual layer is just gone."
  paragraph "This is the closest a sighted developer gets to the experience. It's uncomfortable. That discomfort is information."
  paragraph "While the curtain is on, the panel draws no outline on the page, so use the tree's selection to keep your place."

  heading "Live region monitoring" (level 3)
  paragraph "Only the extension has a live log; the other panels don't watch live regions."
  paragraph "While the side panel is open, when a live region on the page changes or is shown, its new text appears in the live log at the bottom of the side panel. A live region is a , or , or anything with or ; silences even an alert. Each entry shows the region's role ( for an element with no role), then its text. Assertive entries are red, polite ones blue. The log holds the last five entries, and each clears after 8 seconds."

    code "role="log""
    code "role="alert""
    code "aria-live="polite""
    code ""assertive""
    code "aria-live="off""
    code "status"
    code "aria-live"
  paragraph "An entry's text is what a screen reader could announce: nothing hidden or , and never a form field's markup text. Text a region already held when the panel opened isn't logged until it changes. The log approximates a screen reader rather than recording one: each entry carries the region's whole text after the change, not only the part that changed, changes within 200 ms collapse into one entry, and only the top frame is watched, so a live region inside an iframe never appears."
    code "aria-hidden"
